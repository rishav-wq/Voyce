How to not sound like AI on LinkedIn

R
Rishav · Founder of Voyce  ·  Updated August 2026
To not sound like AI on LinkedIn, write from a specific moment, use your own words, and include one concrete detail — a name, a number, a real example. AI-written posts read generic because they open with a thesis, lean on buzzwords, and end on an empty question. Specifics and your real voice are what read as human.

Why does AI-written content all sound the same?

Because most people prompt a raw chatbot the same way — "write me a LinkedIn post about X" — and get the model's average of every post ever written on that topic. The grammar is perfect. That's exactly the problem: it's fluent, tidy, and completely forgettable. Readers can't always name why, but they scroll past it, because it sounds like no one.

The fix isn't better grammar. It's putting you back in — your specifics, your vocabulary, your point of view.

7 tells that scream "an AI wrote this"

  1. The thesis opener. "In today's fast-paced world…" or "Here's what I learned…" A human opens with a moment; AI opens with a summary.
  2. One sentence per line, faux-profound. Every line its own paragraph, each trying to land like a fortune cookie.
  3. Buzzwords doing the work. Leverage, unlock, game-changer, synergy, dive deep. Real people say the plain word.
  4. The "It's not X. It's Y." construction. Once it was clever. Now it's a fingerprint — especially stacked three times in a row.
  5. Tidy tricolons everywhere. Three-part lists, three-word sentences, perfectly balanced. Real writing has uneven edges.
  6. Zero concrete detail. No names, no numbers, no real example you could fact-check. Just smooth, sourceless advice.
  7. The empty closing question. "What do you think?" — bait that invites nothing, because the post never had a real point of view.

How to make your LinkedIn posts sound human again

You don't need to write from scratch. You need to put the human signal back in. Seven fixes, each the inverse of a tell above:

  • Open on a specific moment, not a thesis. "A client asked me this on Tuesday…" beats "Communication is key."
  • Use the words you actually say. If you'd never say "leverage" out loud, don't type it.
  • Put in one checkable specific — a real number, a name, a concrete example. One detail does more than a paragraph of polish.
  • Take a side. A post with an actual opinion reads human; a balanced summary reads like a model hedging.
  • Vary your rhythm. Mix short lines with longer ones. Don't put every sentence on its own line.
  • Cut the buzzwords and the tricolons. Say it once, plainly. Delete the third item in the list.
  • End with something real — a genuine question you'd actually ask, or a clear takeaway. Never engagement bait.

FAQ

Can people really tell when AI writes a LinkedIn post?

Often, yes. The giveaways are pattern, not grammar: a generic opener, one-sentence-per-line drama, buzzwords, a tidy three-part rhythm, and an empty closing question. Readers may not name it, but it reads as forgettable and templated.

How do I make AI-written posts sound like me?

Feed the AI your real writing so it learns your vocabulary and rhythm, then edit for one concrete specific — a name, a number, a real moment — and cut every buzzword. Voice comes from specifics and your actual words, not from better grammar.

Is it bad to use AI for LinkedIn at all?

No — the problem isn't AI, it's generic AI. A tool that writes in a voice learned from your own posts and grounds each post in real, specific facts reads as human. A generic prompt to a raw chatbot is what sounds like everyone else.
